Yes, No, Maybe So

Do voters say yes more than no? Does the way a measure is worded, or what it is about, change the odds?

Read this first: coverage. There is no free, complete, machine-readable database of every statewide measure in all 50 states. This report uses a two-tier design. (1) Measure-level data (2,465 measures) come from Wikipedia's per-state lists, which exist for 15 states and DC (AK, AZ, CA, CO, FL, HI, MI, MO, MT, OH, OK, OR, SD, WA, WY, DC) and are uneven in span. (2) Initiative counts and approval rates for every initiative state come from the Initiative & Referendum Institute (IRI). Everything about wording rests on tier 1 only, so it speaks for those states, not all 50. Ballotpedia's complete data blocks automated access; swapping it in is the obvious next step (see limits).

The short answer

Yes wins slightly more often than no: 57% of measures passed (mean yes share 53.6%). But that average hides a split that matters more than anything else in the data:

  • Who puts it on the ballot matters most. Where origin is identifiable, legislative referrals passed 62% and citizen initiatives only 46%. For initiatives, no wins more than yes. After controls, referrals have about 2.5× the odds.
  • One wording signal is robust: asking voters to repeal, eliminate or abolish something. Those measures passed 37% of the time vs 58%, and the effect survives controls for state, decade, topic and length (odds ratio 0.42; 0.34 in the stricter model).
  • Most other wording features do nothing detectable once state and topic are held fixed: restrictive vs expansive verbs, mentioning taxes or debt, or being a constitutional amendment.
  • Topic matters, but less than origin. Criminal-justice measures pass most (79%); environment, education, social and tax measures sit near 52–54%.
  • State culture matters a lot. Pass rates range from 40% (South Dakota) to 83% (Hawaii). A state's own track record is a strong predictor of any new measure (p<0.001).

1. Do yeses win more than nos?

Across all measures, yes wins 57% of the time, higher than a coin flip (binomial p<0.001). The mean passing measure gets 64% yes; the mean failing one gets 39%. Results are usually decisive: 62% of passes top 60%, and 49% of failures are below 40%.

The pooled number is partly an artifact of mix, though. Legislative referrals, often routine constitutional housekeeping, pass easily. Citizen initiatives, which are contested, usually fail.

3. Does the wording matter?

For each measure I extracted features from the official or summarized title: verbs that restrict (ban, limit, cut) or expand (allow, create, provide), whether it mentions taxes/fees/bonds, an increase, or a repeal; whether it is a constitutional amendment; length; and reading ease (Flesch). I then fit logistic regressions of passing on all of them at once, with state and decade fixed effects and topic controls, so each feature is judged against similar measures in the same state and era.

Model A uses everything. Model B keeps only measures whose origin is known and controls for it. Standard errors cluster by state-election. A bar crossing 1× means the effect is indistinguishable from zero.

  • Repeal language is the one clear signal. Both models agree: odds of passing are roughly 60% lower. Voters appear to resist taking things away, especially when the measure frames itself as undoing an existing law.
  • Restrictive vs expansive framing, tax/debt mentions, "increase" language and amendment status show no reliable effect after controls, even where raw differences look large (for example amendments pass 61% vs 54% raw, but that gap is mostly origin and state).
  • Length and readability are suggestive, not conclusive. Longer text goes with higher pass rates (rank correlation with yes share ρ=0.05). In Model B each extra SD of length raises the odds ~22% and harder-to-read text does slightly better. In Model A neither survives. With eight features tested, treat these as hypotheses.
An important caveat on "wording". Wikipedia's text is a mix of official ballot titles (California, Oregon, Washington) and editors' plain-English summaries (Colorado, Missouri, Wyoming and others). So some "wording" differences are really differences in who wrote the summary. State fixed effects absorb most of this, but a true test of wording effects needs the official ballot language for every measure. That is the main upgrade this project needs.

Hawaii, Florida, DC and Wyoming say yes most often. South Dakota, Oregon, Michigan and Colorado say no almost as often as yes. Compare the very different mean-yes figures: Arizona (45%) and South Dakota (47%) skew skeptical.

State history predicts the next result. Using each measure's own state's pass rate from all other measures (leave-one-out) to predict the outcome gives a coefficient of 3.5 (p<0.001): a state that says yes often keeps saying yes, whatever the wording.

Sanity check against the official initiative record

For the states where I could identify enough initiatives (30+), the pass rate in this dataset closely tracks the IRI's independent count of all initiatives from 1976 to 2024, for example California 40.3% vs 40.4%, Oregon 36.5% vs 35.7%, Washington 53.0% vs 54.0%. That gives some confidence in the parsing and origin tagging. Colorado and Michigan diverge, as their initiative tagging relies on text cues.

Limits and next steps

  • Not all 50 states. Measure-level data cover 15 states and DC; ~24 states allow citizen initiatives but all 50 hold referred measures. Coverage varies: Arizona 1990–2008, Missouri and South Dakota from 2000, Oklahoma from 2004, DC 1979 only. Pooled figures overweight California, Oregon, Colorado and Washington.
  • Origin unknown for 59% of measures. Pass rates by origin use only identifiable cases; Model B uses only those.
  • Topic labels are keyword rules (about 75–80% sensible in a hand audit of 40 titles), so topic effects are blurred toward zero.
  • Wording is measured from summaries, not full ballot language. And "wording" is correlated with substance: a measure to repeal a law is also a different policy, so this is association, not proof that rewording would change outcomes.
  • Outcomes: passage matches simple majority in 98.3% of rows with vote data; exceptions are legitimate supermajority rules (Florida's 60%, Wyoming).
  • Next: add Ballotpedia or IRI measure-level data for all states; add official ballot titles; add a better topic classifier; model state-level covariates (partisanship, signature rules).
