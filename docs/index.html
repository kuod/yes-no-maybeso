Executive summary

Question. Across 2,522 statewide ballot measures (1976–2024, 15 states plus DC), do yeses or nos win more, and do wording, topic, origin or politics change the odds?

  • Yes wins slightly more than no: 57% of measures passed. The average hides a split: legislative referrals passed 63%, citizen initiatives only 46% (about 2.6× the odds after controls). For initiatives, no wins more, though on vote share they split almost exactly 50–50 (50.3% yes).
  • Wording: one robust signal. Measures that ask voters to repeal, eliminate or abolish something pass far less often (odds ratio 0.43). Verb framing, tax mentions and amendment status show nothing reliable for passing once state and topic are held fixed (a few small hints appear in vote share).
  • Type matters, but less than origin. Criminal-justice measures pass most (79%); social, economic, environment and education sit around 52–57%.
  • Campaign spending (exploratory, Washington only): costlier measures did worse, not robustly. Across 42 Washington measures, above-median spenders passed 48% vs 71%, but the estimate is not significant with year controls and spending data are a lower bound (section 6).
  • State history matters a lot, alongside origin. Pass rates run from 40% (South Dakota) to 83% (Hawaii), and a state's record predicts its next result.
  • Politics: no reliable effect. Presidential lean, a state shifting toward either party, governor's party and legislative control do not move the average yes/no once the state is held fixed. Because a "yes" can be conservative or liberal, this does not rule out partisan effects on which measures pass.
  • Hot-button issues: a mild, consistent tilt toward "no", not robust. Measures that draw more voters than others on the same ballot have about 2.6 points lower margins per SD (negative in all eight specifications, significant in some). The test is underpowered, so this is suggestive, not settled.

Confidence and limits. Association, not causation. Wording and topic results cover the 16 jurisdictions with measure-level data, not all 50 states; California has no vote counts; Wikipedia's lists miss some initiatives; topic labels are keyword-based (~75–80% accurate). Two earlier findings were retracted or weakened after audits (a governor-party effect that came from a parser bug, and an over-strong “no hot-button slant”); see section 7.

Read this first: coverage. There is no free, complete, machine-readable database of every statewide measure in all 50 states. This report uses a two-tier design. (1) Measure-level data (2,522 measures) come from Wikipedia's per-state lists, which exist for 15 states and DC (AK, AZ, CA, CO, FL, HI, MI, MO, MT, OH, OK, OR, SD, WA, WY, DC) and are uneven in span. (2) Initiative counts and approval rates for every initiative state come from the Initiative & Referendum Institute (IRI). Everything about wording rests on tier 1 only, so it speaks for those states, not all 50. Ballotpedia's complete data blocks automated access; swapping it in is the obvious next step (see limits).

The short answer

Yes wins slightly more often than no: 57% of measures passed (mean yes share 53.3%). But that average hides a split that matters more than anything else in the data:

  • Who puts it on the ballot matters most. Where origin is identifiable, legislative referrals passed 63% and citizen initiatives only 46%. For initiatives, no wins more than yes. After controls, referrals have about 2.6× the odds.
  • One wording signal is robust: asking voters to repeal, eliminate or abolish something. Those measures passed 38% of the time vs 58%, and the effect survives controls for state, decade, topic and length (odds ratio 0.43; 0.35 in the stricter model).
  • Most other wording features do nothing detectable once state and topic are held fixed: restrictive vs expansive verbs, mentioning taxes or debt, or being a constitutional amendment.
  • Topic matters, but less than origin. Criminal-justice measures pass most (79%); environment, education, social and tax measures sit near 52–54%.
  • Partisan climate does not reliably move the average yes/no. Presidential lean and a state becoming more Democratic or Republican show no reliable effect once the state is held fixed, and neither does legislative control. Democratic-governor years pass a little more often (1.14× the odds), but that is not statistically distinguishable from zero (section 5).
  • Hot-button issues lean slightly toward "no", but not robustly. Measures that draw more voters than others on the same ballot have somewhat lower margins and pass less often (about -2.6 points and 0.86× the odds per SD), consistent in sign across eight specifications but significant in only some, and the test is underpowered (section 6, explorer).
  • State culture matters a lot. Pass rates range from 40% (South Dakota) to 83% (Hawaii). A state's own track record is a strong predictor of any new measure (p<0.001).

1. Do yeses win more than nos?

Across all measures, yes wins 57% of the time, higher than a coin flip (binomial p<0.001). The mean passing measure gets 64% yes; the mean failing one gets 39%. Results are usually decisive: 62% of passes top 60%, and 49% of failures are below 40%.

The yes/no view tells a slightly different story. Citizen initiatives draw a mean yes share of 50.3% (95% CI 48.8–51.7), which is an even split, even though only 46% of them pass (the average vote is not a rejection). Legislative referrals average 55.1% yes. Repeal language costs about 5.3 points of yes share (the strongest wording effect in either view, p=0.002). The yes-share view also nudges a few other features: tax/debt mentions -2.1 points (p=0.026), constitutional amendments +2.4 (p=0.017) and longer text +1.0 per SD (p=0.079). With eight features tested, only repeal survives a Bonferroni correction (p<0.006); treat the rest as hints.

The pooled number is partly an artifact of mix, though. Legislative referrals, often routine constitutional housekeeping, pass easily. Citizen initiatives, which are contested, usually fail.

Two views of every figure. Passed or failed asks whether yes cleared the bar. Yes or no asks how the vote went: the mean share of ballots cast that were yes, so a measure that lost 49–51 and one that lost 20–80 count the same in the first view and very differently in the second. Vote shares exist for every measure except California's (no vote counts) and a few others, so the yes-share panels use fewer measures; the n is in each hover label. Hawaii's share counts blank ballots as "no", which pulls its yes share down. Where supermajorities apply (FL, WY), a measure can pass with under 50% yes.

3. Does the wording matter?

For each measure I extracted features from the official or summarized title: verbs that restrict (ban, limit, cut) or expand (allow, create, provide), whether it mentions taxes/fees/bonds, an increase, or a repeal; whether it is a constitutional amendment; length; and reading ease (Flesch). I then fit logistic regressions of passing on all of them at once, with state and decade fixed effects and topic controls, so each feature is judged against similar measures in the same state and era.

Model A uses everything. Model B keeps only measures whose origin is known and controls for it. Standard errors cluster by state-election. A bar crossing 1× means the effect is indistinguishable from zero.

  • Repeal language is the one clear signal. Both models agree: odds of passing are roughly 60% lower. Voters appear to resist taking things away, especially when the measure frames itself as undoing an existing law.
  • Restrictive vs expansive framing, tax/debt mentions, "increase" language and amendment status show no reliable effect on passing after controls (the yes-share panel above shows small hints for tax/debt and amendments), even where raw differences look large (for example amendments pass 61% vs 54% raw, but that gap is mostly origin and state).
  • Length and readability are suggestive, not conclusive. Longer text goes with higher pass rates (rank correlation with yes share ρ=0.05). In Model B each extra SD of length raises the odds ~22% and harder-to-read text does slightly better. In Model A neither survives. With eight features tested, treat these as hypotheses.
An important caveat on "wording". Wikipedia's text is a mix of official ballot titles (California, Oregon, Washington) and editors' plain-English summaries (Colorado, Missouri, Wyoming and others). So some "wording" differences are really differences in who wrote the summary. State fixed effects absorb most of this, but a true test of wording effects needs the official ballot language for every measure. That is the main upgrade this project needs.

For the states where I could identify enough initiatives (30+), the pass rate in this dataset closely tracks the IRI's independent count of all initiatives from 1976 to 2024, for example California 40.3% vs 40.4%, Oregon 36.5% vs 35.7%, Washington 53.0% vs 54.0%. 5. Does politics matter? Presidential lean, governors and legislatures

To test whether a state's partisan climate affects yes-or-no outcomes, I attached four pieces of context to every measure (DC is excluded because it has no governor or state legislature):

  • Lean: the Democratic two-party margin in the most recent presidential race before the measure's year (a same-year race is simultaneous, not history).
  • Shift: how far that margin moved from the race before it. Positive means the state was becoming more Democratic; negative, more Republican. I also tested a two-cycle (8-year) shift.
  • Governor's party and legislative control (both chambers Democratic, both Republican, or split/tied) as shown for that year in Wikipedia's party-strength tables. A Democratic or Republican trifecta means governor plus both chambers.

With controls: mostly no detectable effect

The cleaner test uses state fixed effects, so lean and shift are identified by the same state changing over time rather than comparing, say, Wyoming with California. It also controls for decade and topic and clusters by state-election.

  • A state becoming more Democratic or more Republican shows no reliable effect (odds ratio 0.92 per 10 points toward Democrats, p=0.25; the 8-year version is similar). The point estimate leans slightly negative, so the raw gap above is not distinguishable from noise.
  • Governor's party shows a small tilt that is not statistically reliable. Democratic-governor years pass slightly more often, but the pass/fail odds ratio (1.14, CI 0.89–1.46, p=0.31) is not significant, and the odds ratio stays between 1.06 and 1.29 whichever state is dropped. Within states the direction is mostly the same: more passes under Democratic governors in 10 of the 12 states with enough data (average +5.5 points), though Arizona and Missouri go the other way. Why the null result isn't surprising. A "yes" vote is not inherently partisan: it can enact a conservative or a liberal policy. If Democratic-leaning electorates say yes to a minimum-wage hike and no to a tax cap while Republican ones do the opposite, the pass rate averaged across all measures cancels out. So these results show that a state's partisan climate does not make voters generally more or less willing to say yes. They say nothing about whether partisanship shapes which measures pass. Testing that needs each measure coded for its ideological direction, which this dataset does not yet have (see next steps).

6. Hot-button issues: do the most-voted measures lean yes or no?

Open the interactive Hot-Button Explorer → sort every measure by votes per capita, filter by state, topic, origin and era, and see the margin for or against on the y-axis.

I measured how many voters weighed in on each measure (yes plus no votes) and divided by the state's population that year. Three versions, because the obvious one is misleading:

  • Raw votes per capita mostly measures which election it was. In presidential years the median is 404 voters per 1,000 residents vs 293 otherwise, and 96% of the top-decile measures sit in presidential years (they are 43% of all measures). The highest raw value of all is 551 per 1,000 (Montana, 2020).
  • Engagement vs the same ballot divides a measure's votes by the most-voted measure on that state-year's ballot (ballots with at least 5 measures), which removes the election effect.
  • Engagement beyond ballot position additionally removes the small trend with where the measure sits on the ballot.

  • A modest, consistent tilt toward "no" as engagement rises, but not a robust one. The raw and election-adjusted views lean slightly toward "no" (the most-engaged fifth averages about +3 points vs +10 for the least-engaged). Comparing measures on the same ballot, the baseline estimate is -2.6 points per standard deviation of engagement (95% CI -5.8 to +0.6, p=0.11). Across the eight variants I tried, the estimate is never positive (-3.5 to -1.7 points; pass odds 0.84–0.91). It strengthens when the electorates are cleaner: counting only even-year, non-special ballots gives -3.5 points (p=0.001; pass odds 0.84, p=0.003), and only ballots whose measures all drew similar vote counts gives -2.2 (p=0.06; pass odds 0.88, p=0.04). Because I tried eight variants, treat the two significant ones as suggestive. The ballot-position-adjusted version is weaker (-1.7, p=0.28) and its fifths do not slope downward (+7.5 vs +8.4).
  • The test is underpowered. With these standard errors the smallest slant I could reliably detect is about 4.6 points per standard deviation, larger than every estimate above. So "not significant" here does not mean "no slant"; a real tilt of the size observed would usually go undetected.
  • Hot is not the same as close. More-engaged measures are not detectably more narrowly decided (margin size changes -0.8 points per SD, p=0.36).
  • Passing odds: within a ballot, more-engaging measures pass less often in every variant (odds ratio 0.86 per SD in the baseline, p=0.06), consistent with a mild status-quo bias: when more people vote, "no" gains a little.
  • Which topics draw voters? Social-issue measures draw the most voters relative to their ballot, though the spread across topic areas is small (below).

Exploratory: does campaign spending change the trend? (Washington only)

The natural follow-up is whether expensive campaigns move yes or no. I could test it for one state only: Washington's Public Disclosure Commission publishes open campaign-finance data. I matched committee spending to 42 Washington initiatives and referendums (2007–2024; $253M in total; median $2.2M per measure).

  • Higher-spending measures did worse, but not robustly. Measures above the median spend passed 48% of the time with a mean margin of -1.4 points, vs 71% and +10.8 below it. The rank correlation is -0.34 (p=0.03), but it is not robust to the same-year comparison: shuffling spending only among measures on the same year's ballot gives p=0.19, and with year fixed effects the slope shrinks and the interval spans zero. A plausible reading is that money flows to contested measures (reverse causation), not that spending causes losses.
  • The side with more money won more often (83% vs 37%, p=0.01), but I would not rely on this. Assigning committees to the yes or no side relied partly on their names.
Why this is only exploratory. Of 297 statewide-ballot committees active in 2007–2024, only 154 name the measure they work on, either in a data field or in their own name (for example "Yes on 1183"). The 143 others spent $119M vs $272M for the attributed ones, so every spending figure here is a lower bound, and for/against totals are unreliable (committees are assigned to the yes or no side partly from their names, and the default is yes). 3 of 45 initiatives and referendums have no matched committee and are left out; 5 matched ones reported $0 and are counted as zero. It is one state with a small sample, and spending was not randomly assigned. Answering the question properly needs a per-measure spending source across many states.
Two caveats specific to this section. California is missing. It has no vote counts in my data: the Secretary of State publishes them as per-election PDFs on a CDN that rejects ordinary automated requests, and I have not pulled them. It holds many of the best-known hot-button measures (Props 13, 187, 209, 8, 64). Everything here covers the other 14 states and DC, 1,941 measures. Votes per capita is not turnout: the denominator is total resident population, so it understates participation and varies with age structure and non-citizens.

7. Stress tests: what I tried to break

After the first version of this report I went looking for ways the headline results could be wrong. What I found and what held up:

  • A bug in two wording features, now fixed. The "expansive verbs" pattern contained amend, so it fired on nearly every constitutional amendment (correlation with "amendment" 0.62), and "restrictive" matched words like capital and bargaining. After fixing both (correlation now 0.13), every wording estimate moved by a few hundredths at most and no conclusion changed, but the earlier "restrictive vs expansive" comparison did not measure what it claimed.
  • The repeal effect holds up. Dropping any one state leaves the odds ratio between 0.34 and 0.49 (all p<0.002). It appears in 13 states and within each origin: initiatives with repeal language passed 25% vs 47% without; referrals 45% vs 64%. Caveats: it is only 101 measures, the word's presence is not the same as the measure's main action, and a repeal measure is also a different policy.
  • The origin gap is not an Oregon artifact, but the data are lopsided. Oregon supplies 45% of identified legislative referrals, and 30% of all origin labels are inferred from description text. Restricting to the 6 states with at least 10 of each origin (Arizona, California, Montana, Oregon, South Dakota, Washington) and comparing within state, referrals still have 2.4× the odds (CI 1.6–3.6). The direction is reversed or flat in a few small-sample states (Michigan, South Dakota).
  • The lists are incomplete. Against the IRI's official initiative counts, Wikipedia's lists are short by at least 106 initiatives in 50 of 295 state-years. Most of that is the late-starting windows noted above (Missouri, Oklahoma, South Dakota); the rest are unexplained gaps in Arizona, Montana, Ohio and Oregon (about 35 initiatives). I cannot tell whether missing measures skew toward passes or failures; this is a lower bound only for initiatives, and referrals cannot be checked this way.
  • Partisan results under harsher inference. Clustering by state (15 clusters) instead of by state-election changes none of the logit conclusions. Removing decade fixed effects makes shifts toward Democrats look slightly more negative (odds ratio 0.93, p=0.07) but still not significant.
  • A parser bug changed a conclusion. While adding vote counts I found that Arizona's stacked-header tables for 2010–2024 had been silently skipped (57 measures). Restoring them moved the governor-party yes-share effect from +2.6 points (bootstrap p≈0.006, which I had reported as the most credible partisan signal) to +1.5 points (p=0.20). Other headline results moved by less than a point. Lesson: a result that hinges on a handful of states is only as good as the audit of each state's rows.
  • The first hot-button conclusion was too strong. I first wrote "no reliable slant." A sensitivity check showed that state-year "ballots" sometimes mix a primary or special election with the general (about 6% of ballots have vote counts differing by more than 2×), which attenuates the engagement measure, and that the test could only detect effects above ~5 points per SD. The conclusion was revised to "a consistent but not robust tilt toward no" (section 6).
  • Two errors caught in the hot-button analysis before publishing. A helper that returned the wrong regression coefficient (it reported a dummy variable, producing spurious p<0.001 results), and a "ballot position" variable that was accidentally each measure's own engagement rank (correlation −0.76 with engagement; the real correlation is +0.14). Both are fixed; the section 6 numbers use the corrected code.
  • Not tested: causation; whether Wikipedia's coverage is selective; independent double-extraction of the governor and legislature tables; and ideological direction of each measure. All results are associations in 15 states.

Limits and next steps

  • Not all 50 states. Measure-level data cover 15 states and DC; ~24 states allow citizen initiatives but all 50 hold referred measures. Coverage varies: Arizona from 1990, Missouri and South Dakota from 2000, Oklahoma from 2004, Montana and Ohio from 1980, DC 1979 only. Pooled figures overweight California, Oregon, Colorado and Washington.
  • Origin unknown for 59% of measures. Pass rates by origin use only identifiable cases, and Oregon supplies nearly half of the identified referrals (section 7). Model B uses only those cases.
  • Lists are incomplete. At least 106 initiatives from the IRI's official count are absent (section 7), and referral coverage cannot be checked.
  • Topic labels are keyword rules (about 75–80% sensible in a hand audit of 40 titles), so topic effects are blurred toward zero.
  • Wording is measured from summaries, not full ballot language. And "wording" is correlated with substance: a measure to repeal a law is also a different policy, so this is association, not proof that rewording would change outcomes.
  • Outcomes: passage matches simple majority in 98.5% of rows with vote data; exceptions are legitimate supermajority rules (Florida's 60%, Wyoming).
  • Partisan context: governor and legislature data were extracted from Wikipedia's party-strength pages by four parallel agents, then checked for row counts, chamber sizes and 17 known state-years. Treat individual cells as probably right, not audited. Ties are coded as split. Years are as shown on the page, with the governor in office that year. Only 15 states, so partisan estimates are noisy. Without ideological coding of each measure, the partisan analysis cannot say whether liberal or conservative measures fare differently by climate.
  • Next (also): code each measure's ideological direction (conservative, liberal, neutral) to test climate-by-direction effects, the natural follow-up to section 5.
  • Next: add Ballotpedia or IRI measure-level data for all states; add official ballot titles; add a better topic classifier; model state-level covariates (partisanship, signature rules).
